Earth's Layers

Drill all the way to the center of the Earth, and find out why the hottest place down there is solid.

1 Nobody has ever been there

The deepest hole people have ever dug is the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia. It took about 20 years to drill, and it goes down about 12 kilometers. That sounds deep, but the center of the Earth is about 6,371 kilometers down.

So how do we know what's inside? Mostly from earthquake waves. When a big earthquake happens, its waves travel through the whole planet. They speed up, slow down and bend when they hit different materials, and one kind of wave can't get through liquid at all. By listening to the waves on the other side of the world, scientists mapped the inside of the Earth in layers.

3 Hotter and more squeezed

Two things kept going up the whole way down. It got hotter, because the Earth still holds heat left over from when it formed, plus heat from radioactive rock. And the pressure went up, because every kilometer you go down adds more rock on top of you pressing down.

The mantle is solid rock, but it's so hot that over millions of years it slowly flows, like very thick putty. That slow flow is what moves the continents (that's lesson 4).

Here's the weird part: the outer core is liquid metal, but the inner core, which is even hotter, is solid. Keep that puzzle in your head.

4 The Melt-o-meter

Something melts when it's hotter than its melting point. Ice melts above 0 °C. Iron at the surface melts at about 1,538 °C. But squeezing changes the melting point: when atoms are pressed tight together, they need more heat to break loose and flow.
