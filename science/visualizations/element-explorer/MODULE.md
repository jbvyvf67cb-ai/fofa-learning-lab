## Lesson 1 — What's inside an atom?
**Goal:** name the three particles and where they live.

An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element. It has three parts:
- **Protons** and **neutrons** sit together in the **nucleus** — a tiny, dense clump at the center.
- **Electrons** zip around the outside in layers called **shells**.

The nucleus is incredibly small but holds almost all the atom's mass. The rest of the atom
is mostly empty space.

**Key words:** atom, nucleus, proton, neutron, electron, electron shell.
**Common mistake:** electrons are *not* inside the nucleus — they're outside, in shells.
**Try it:** Add and remove each particle; protons/neutrons land in the center, electrons on the rings.

**Check yourself:**
1. Which two particles are in the nucleus? _A: protons and neutrons._
2. Which particle moves around the outside? _A: the electron._
3. True/False: most of an atom is empty space. _A: True._

## Lesson 2 — Charge: neutral atoms vs. ions
**Goal:** explain charge and how atoms become ions.

Charges: proton **+1**, electron **−1**, neutron **0**. In a normal atom, protons = electrons,
so charges cancel → **neutral** (0).

**Charge = protons − electrons.** Lose electrons → **positive (cation)**; gain electrons →
**negative (anion)**. A charged atom is an **ion**. In chemistry, only **electrons** move — never protons.

**Key words:** charge, neutral, ion, cation (+), anion (−).
**Common mistake:** thinking you remove protons to make a positive ion — you remove electrons.
**Try it:** Keep protons fixed, change electrons; watch the charge and the Na⁺/Cl⁻ label.

**Check yourself:**
1. 11 protons, 10 electrons → charge? _A: +1._
2. An atom gains 2 electrons — cation or anion? _A: anion (negative)._
3. What is an ion? _A: an atom with a charge (unequal protons and electrons)._

## Lesson 3 — Mass and mass number
**Goal:** know what gives an atom its mass.

Protons and neutrons each weigh about **1 amu**; electrons weigh almost nothing (~1/1836).
So mass comes from the nucleus: **mass number A = protons + neutrons**. Electrons don't count.

**Key words:** atomic mass unit (amu), mass number (A).
**Common mistake:** counting electrons for mass, or confusing mass number with atomic number.
**Try it:** Watch "Mass number (A = Z + N)" — it changes with protons/neutrons but not electrons.

**Check yourself:**
1. 6 protons, 8 neutrons → mass number? _A: 14._
2. Why ignore electrons for mass? _A: they weigh almost nothing._
3. Which particles give almost all the mass? _A: protons and neutrons._

## Lesson 4 — Atomic number: the element's ID badge
**Goal:** connect proton count to element identity.

The **number of protons = atomic number (Z)**, and it decides **which element** it is. Change
protons → change element. Hydrogen = 1, Carbon = 6, Oxygen = 8. The periodic table is ordered
by atomic number. In a neutral atom, electrons also equal Z.

**Key words:** atomic number (Z), element, chemical symbol.
**Common mistake:** thinking neutrons or electrons decide the element — only protons do.
**Try it:** Drag the proton slider; the element name/symbol change.

**Check yourself:**
1. What determines the element? _A: number of protons (atomic number)._
2. 8 protons → which element? _A: oxygen._
3. Add a proton to carbon (6) → what forms? _A: nitrogen (7)._

## Lesson 5 — Isotopes (intro)
**Goal:** define isotopes. *(Full treatment + average mass is in Module 2.)*

Same element = **same protons, always**. But the neutron count can differ → **isotopes**
(Carbon‑12 vs Carbon‑14). Same chemistry, different mass. Named element‑massnumber.

**Key words:** isotope.
**Common mistake:** different neutrons makes a different element — no, a different *isotope*.
**Try it:** Lock protons, change neutrons; the isotope name and mass number change.

**Check yourself:**
1. Carbon‑12 and Carbon‑14 share the same number of what? _A: protons (and electrons)._
2. What differs between two isotopes? _A: the number of neutrons._
3. Oxygen‑18 has 8 protons — how many neutrons? _A: 10._

## Lesson 6 — A balanced nucleus (stability)
**Goal:** explain why atoms need a balance of neutrons and protons.

Protons all repel each other (like charges push apart). **Neutrons act like glue** — they add
a strong short‑range pull (the **strong nuclear force**) with no extra push. Too few or too
many neutrons → **unstable**. Stable atoms form a **band of stability**: small atoms ≈ equal
p and n; big atoms need **more neutrons than protons**.

**Key words:** stable, unstable, band of stability, strong nuclear force.
**Common mistake:** more neutrons = always more stable. There's a "just right" range.
**Try it:** Change neutrons; watch the badge (nature/lab/theoretical/impossible) and the N‑vs‑Z chart.

**Check yourself:**
1. What holds the nucleus together against proton repulsion? _A: the strong nuclear force._
2. Why do neutrons help? _A: they add pull without adding positive push._
3. Do heavy atoms need more or fewer neutrons than protons? _A: more._

## Lesson 7 — Radioactive decay (high level)
**Goal:** describe what an unstable nucleus does.

An **unstable (radioactive)** nucleus **decays** over time, giving off particles/energy
(**radiation**) to become more stable. Simple types:
- **Alpha (α):** ejects 2 protons + 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus).
- **Beta (β):** a neutron turns into a proton and emits an electron.
- **Gamma (γ):** releases energy only.

**Half-life** = time for **half** the atoms in a sample to decay (random, gradual — not all at
once). Examples: Carbon‑14 dating; uranium over billions of years.

**Key words:** radioactive, radiation, decay, half-life, alpha/beta/gamma.
**Common mistake:** a radioactive sample decays all at once/instantly — no, gradually, by half-life.
**Try it:** Build Carbon‑14 (6p, 8n) vs stable Carbon‑12; note radioactive labeling.

**Check yourself:**
1. What is a half-life? _A: the time for half the atoms in a sample to decay._
2. In beta decay a neutron becomes a ___? _A: a proton (plus an emitted electron)._
3. Alpha decay releases a particle made of? _A: 2 protons + 2 neutrons._

## Bonus — How our picture of the atom changed
- **Dalton (~1803):** atoms are tiny solid balls.
- **Thomson (~1897):** found **electrons**; "plum pudding" model.
- **Rutherford (~1911):** gold‑foil experiment → a tiny dense **nucleus**.
- **Bohr (~1913):** electrons in fixed **shells / energy levels**.
- **Modern:** electrons live in fuzzy **clouds** (probabilities, not exact paths).

**Check yourself:** Who discovered the nucleus? _A: Rutherford._ · Who discovered the electron? _A: Thomson._
