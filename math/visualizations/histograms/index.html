Histograms & the Shape of Data

With a handful of numbers you can list them all. With thousands, you bin them into bars — and the shape tells a story.

A histogram sorts data into bins (ranges) and draws a bar for each — the taller the bar, the more values landed in that bin. Let's build one from something we already understand: the sum of two dice.

1 Build a histogram by counting

Each bar is one possible sum (2 to 12). Every roll drops into its bar. Roll a few — it's lumpy. Roll thousands — a clear shape emerges.

Press Roll ×1000 a few times and watch the bars settle into a shape.

2 The shape is the Lesson-3 triangle

That peak at 7 with slopes down to 2 and 12 is exactly the counting picture from Lesson 3 — 7 has the most ways to make it, 2 and 12 the fewest. A histogram of real rolls grows into the theory we counted. This hump-in-the-middle shape is called symmetric (or "bell-shaped") — common whenever lots of small random things add up.

3 Not everything is symmetric — skew

Many real data sets lean to one side. If most values are small with a few big ones trailing off to the right (like "number of pets per family"), we call it right-skewed. The reverse is left-skewed. The shape instantly tells you where the data piles up.
