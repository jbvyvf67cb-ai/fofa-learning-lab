Sums & Permutations

Roll two dice and add them up. Why is 7 so easy and 2 so hard? The 36-grid shows exactly why.

Here's the same 36-square grid from Lesson 2 — every equally-likely roll of two dice. This time we care about the total (die A + die B). Some totals turn out way more likely than others, and the grid shows us why, just by counting squares.

1 Mark the squares that make a total

Pick a total, then click every square that adds up to it. Green means you found one; a wrong square shakes. Find them all!

2 Why order matters: permutations

Look at the squares you marked. (1,5) and (5,1) are different squares — in one, die A showed 1; in the other, die A showed 5. They're genuinely different rolls, so we count them both. These ordered arrangements have a name: permutations.

Total:
The marked squares are mirror images across the diagonal (the dotted line): every (a,b) has a partner (b,a) — except a "doubles" square like 3,3, which is its own mirror. Counting both of each mirror pair is what keeps all 36 squares equally likely.

3 Where the shape comes from

Now colour the grid by total. Every square on the same slanted stripe has the same sum. The height of each bar below is just how many squares are on that stripe. That's the whole secret: the famous "bell" shape is nothing but counting equally-likely squares.

Highlight total:
P(total) = (squares that make it) ÷ 36. "Chaining" two dice didn't need any new magic — the probability of a total is just its share of the flat 36-square grid. Count the squares, divide by 36. Done.

4 Check yourself

You now have the one idea everything else is built on: probability is counting outcomes in the sample space. From here we can tackle coins, "at least one", combinations, and real data.
