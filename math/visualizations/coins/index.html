Coins & “At Least One”

A coin is just a 2-sided die — but it's the perfect tool for three new moves: the flip-it-around trick, a sample space that doubles, and “at least one”.

A coin has two outcomes — Heads or Tails — each with probability 1/2. That's all it is: the simplest possible random thing, a die with two faces. But that simplicity lets us learn three powerful ideas the dice couldn't show us easily.

1 The flip-it-around trick (the complement)

Here's the coin's sample space — just two outcomes:

Since something must happen, the chances of all outcomes always add up to 1 (that's 100%). So if you know the chance of a thing, you instantly know the chance of it not happening — just subtract from 1:

P(not A) = 1 − P(A)  — the complement rule.

This works for anything, not just coins. Pick an event on a die below (the green faces). The green part is your event; the red part is "not it". They always fill the whole bar — together they make 1.

2 Each coin doubles the outcomes (a tree)

With two dice we drew a 6×6 grid — but that only worked because it was flat (two dice = two directions). Coins let us keep going: each new coin just splits every outcome in two — Heads or Tails — so the sample space grows as a tree.

Number of coins:
Follow any path from the top down to a leaf — that's one possible result, like H,T,H. Count the leaves at the bottom: that's the whole sample space.
Each coin doubles the outcomes: 2 → 4 → 8 → 16. That's the same “multiply” rule as the dice grid (2 × 2 × 2 …), now drawn as a tree. With N coins there are 2N equally-likely sequences.

3 “At least one” — the trick pays off

Now a question that looks hard: flip several coins — what's the chance of getting at least one Heads? Listing every winning sequence is a pain. But flip it around: the only way to fail is to get no Heads at all (every coin Tails) — that's just one sequence. So:

P(at least one Heads) = 1 − P(no Heads) = 1 − (1/2)N

Notice how fast it climbs toward certain: with more coins, getting zero Heads becomes almost impossible, so "at least one" becomes almost sure. Counting the one way to lose and subtracting is far easier than counting all the ways to win.
